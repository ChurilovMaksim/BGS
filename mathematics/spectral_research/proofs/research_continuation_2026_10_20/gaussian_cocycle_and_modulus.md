# Gaussian contour reduction, dominated cocycle, and modulus estimate

## Scope and status

This note independently checks the Gaussian reduction and its consequences. It proves the contour deformation, the trace-class reduction, the global normalized graph construction, the exact cancellation of mixed products, the summed remainder for every positive iterate, and the modulus estimate.

The proof has two external inputs, which must be proved for the actual quantization rather than inferred from semiclassical plausibility:

1. **Exact sector input.** The finite quantum trace is the asserted finite sum of full, Gaussian-regularized Fresnel integrals over `R^m`, with the stated odd-lattice scalar and with sector representatives indexed by the appropriate deck classes. This note proves the deformation and factorization of each such integral. It does not replace the separate proof that these integrals give the actual finite quantum trace.
2. **Exact amplitude input.** On a fixed spatial complex strip, the exact amplitude has uniform spatial analytic bounds and uniform real-h regularity through order two. Its first two coefficients are

   $$
   B_h(Q,q)=B_0(Q,q)+ih\,b_1(Q,q)+O(h^2),
   \qquad B_0=2\sqrt{F_{Qq}},
   $$

   where `B_0` and `b_1` are real on the real domain, and the remainder is uniform in a smaller analytic strip. The square root is the positive branch. The amplitude audit supplies this input, including the symmetry `B_{-h}=overline{B_h}` on the real domain.

Spatial analyticity and a bound `B_h=1+O(t)` by themselves do **not** imply the second input. Likewise, trace-class contour operators by themselves do **not** imply the first input. Subject to these explicitly separated inputs, the local reduction below has no remaining all-time gap.

Write

$$
\lambda=3+2\sqrt2,
\qquad \rho=\lambda^{-1}=3-2\sqrt2,
\qquad \lambda_0=\frac{2\sqrt\pi}{\sqrt\lambda},
\qquad t=\frac{10^{-80}}{2\pi}.
$$

The real generating function is

$$
F(Q,q)=F_0(Q,q)+G(Q,q),
\qquad
F_0(Q,q)=-\frac{3Q^2-2Qq+3q^2}{8}.
$$

The perturbation `G` is bounded and holomorphic in a fixed strip, real on the real domain, and small there. A sufficient quantitative phase estimate used below is

$$
\sup |\partial_i\partial_jG|\le\eta,
\qquad \eta\le 2\cdot10^7|t|.
$$

All bounds are uniform over real base points. The map `Psi` is the invertible real symplectic map generated by `F`, with

$$
p=-F_q(Q,q),\qquad P=F_Q(Q,q),\qquad \Psi(q,p)=(Q,P).
$$

## 1. A globally defined centered edge kernel

For an edge based at `x=(q,p)`, with `Psi x=(Q,P)`, define

$$
\widetilde F_x(z',z)
=F(Q+z',q+z)-F(Q,q)-Pz'+pz.
\tag{1.1}
$$

Thus the constant and both linear Taylor coefficients vanish. Its quadratic part is one half of the Hessian of `F` at the edge.

The needed deck law is the exact identity

$$
F(Q+a,q+b)-F(Q,q)
=cQ-dq+\frac{ca-db}{2},
\qquad (a,c)=L(b,d),
\tag{1.2}
$$

and the amplitude obeys

$$
B_h(Q+a,q+b)=B_h(Q,q).
\tag{1.3}
$$

Differentiating (1.2) gives `P -> P+c` and `p -> p+d`. Applying (1.2) once at the displaced point and once at the base point shows

$$
\widetilde F_{x+(b,d)}(z',z)=\widetilde F_x(z',z).
\tag{1.4}
$$

The terms `cz'-dz` from the deck law cancel the changes in the removed linear jets. Consequently, the centered kernels below are genuine functions on the torus with values in operators on one fixed Hilbert space. There is no chart-dependent phase left in this operator cocycle.

For a lifted periodic trajectory of length `m`, write

$$
(q_m,p_m)=(q_0+n_q,p_0+n_p).
$$

The sector phase is

$$
\mathcal S(q_0,\ldots,q_{m-1})
=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}F(q_{j+1},q_j)
-n_p(q_0+n_q/2),
\qquad q_m=q_0+n_q.
\tag{1.5}
$$

Set each integration variable equal to its critical value plus `z_j`, with `z_m=z_0`. The edge linear terms telescope to

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(p_{j+1}z_{j+1}-p_jz_j)
=n_pz_0.
$$

This cancels the linear term from the last summand of (1.5). Therefore, exactly, not only to a finite Taylor order,

$$
\mathcal S-\mathcal S_{\rm crit}
=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\widetilde F_{x_j}(z_{j+1},z_j).
\tag{1.6}
$$

## 2. A bounded contour with global coercivity

Choose a smooth odd function `psi` such that

$$
\psi(u)=u\text{ near }0,
\qquad 0\le\psi'(u)\le1,
$$

and `psi` is a nonzero constant on each sufficiently far positive or negative tail. Its absolute value stays strictly inside the available analytic strip. Such a function is obtained by integrating a smooth, even cutoff that is one near zero and zero outside a slightly larger interval.

The slope at zero must be exactly one for the Gaussian normalization used here. Merely saying that the contour is linear near zero is insufficient to identify the displayed `K_G`.

Put

$$
z_s(y)=sy-i\psi(sy),\qquad s=\sqrt h>0,
\qquad c=1-i.
\tag{2.1}
$$

For two real variables write `u'=sy'`, `u=sy`, `v'=psi(u')`, `v=psi(u)`, and

$$
D=u'v'+uv\ge0.
$$

For the unperturbed quadratic phase, direct calculation gives

$$
\operatorname{Re}\bigl(iF_0(u'-iv',u-iv)\bigr)
=-\frac{3D-(u'v+uv')}{4}.
\tag{2.2}
$$

Monotonicity gives

$$
(u'-u)(\psi(u')-\psi(u))\ge0,
$$

so `u'v+uv' <= D`. Hence

$$
\operatorname{Re}(iF_0)\le-\frac12D.
\tag{2.3}
$$

This inequality remains coercive in the saturated tails. The fact that the imaginary part of the contour is bounded does not remove decay: the quadratic phase then gives decay exponential in the absolute value of the real coordinate.

Here is a bound for the centered perturbation that does not mistakenly replace tail decay by a quadratic estimate. Let `g` be the centered version of `G`, let `U=(u',u)`, and let `V=(v',v)`. Since `G` is real on real arguments,

$$
\operatorname{Im}g(U-iV)
=-\int_0^1
\bigl(\operatorname{Re}\nabla G(X+U-i\theta V)-\nabla G(X)\bigr)\cdot V\,d\theta.
$$

Using the uniform Hessian bound, `|V_i|<=|U_i|`, and monotonicity of `psi` on nonnegative arguments gives

$$
|\operatorname{Im}g(U-iV)|
\le \eta\,(|U|_1+|V|_1)|V|_1
\le4\eta D.
\tag{2.4}
$$

The last step uses

$$
(|u'|+|u|)(|v'|+|v|)
\le2(|u'v'|+|uv|).
$$

For the stated tiny `eta`, (2.3) and (2.4) imply the convenient uniform estimate

$$
\operatorname{Re}
\frac{i\widetilde F_x(z_s(y'),z_s(y))}{s^2}
\le-\frac{D}{4s^2}.
\tag{2.5}
$$

There is an `a>0`, independent of `s` and the base point, for which

$$
\frac{u\psi(u)}{s^2}
\ge
\begin{cases}
y^2,& |sy|\le a,\\
a|y|/s,& |sy|\ge a.
\end{cases}
\tag{2.6}
$$

The first line follows from the straight portion of the contour, and the second from monotonicity. All edge kernels therefore have integrable tails. In a cyclic product every variable occurs in two edge bounds, so the product integral is absolutely convergent in every fixed dimension `m`.

## 3. The deformation of the full Fresnel integral

The relevant sector is the full integral over `R^m`, not an integral restricted to a fundamental cube. Introduce Gaussian regularization

$$
e^{-\varepsilon\sum_j u_j^2},\qquad \varepsilon>0,
$$

in centered real coordinates. Use its entire continuation during the contour deformation.

For fixed `epsilon`, `h`, and `m`, Cauchy's theorem deforms each real coordinate to the graph `u-i psi(u)`. One can first truncate all coordinates to finite intervals and use the vertical segments joining the endpoints. On those side faces the imaginary coordinate stays bounded. The quadratic phase grows in absolute modulus by at most `exp(C_{h,m}R)` when all real coordinates are in a box of radius `R`. The perturbation and the amplitude have the corresponding strip bounds. The regulator supplies `exp(-epsilon R^2)`, so every boundary contribution tends to zero. Sequential independent coordinate deformations are legitimate for the same reason.

On the final product contour the modulus of the regulator is

$$
\exp\left[-\varepsilon\sum_j
(u_j^2-\psi(u_j)^2)\right]\le1,
$$

with the above choice `|psi(u)|<=|u|`. Estimate (2.5) supplies an integrable majorant independent of `epsilon` for `0<epsilon<=1`. Dominated convergence therefore removes the regulator on the deformed side.

It follows that the Gaussian-regularized real Fresnel integral equals the absolutely convergent contour integral. No unaccounted boundary contribution remains. This argument proves an identity for each finite `m`; it does not require a uniform stationary-phase expansion in dimension `m`.

If the chosen definition of the original sector uses uncentered rather than centered Gaussian regularization, the identical argument applies with its shifted entire regulator. Its final-contour modulus is bounded by an `epsilon`-independent constant for `0<epsilon<=1`, and the same dominated limit results.

## 4. Trace-class kernels and the Gaussian limit

Assign the contour Jacobian to the input variable and set

$$
J_s(y)=\frac{1-i\psi'(sy)}{1-i}.
$$

Define the operator on `H=L^2(R)` by its kernel

$$
k_s(x;y',y)
=\lambda_0^{-1}
B_{s^2}(Q+z_s(y'),q+z_s(y))
J_s(y)
\exp\left(
\frac{i\widetilde F_x(z_s(y'),z_s(y))}{s^2}
\right).
\tag{4.1}
$$

The normalization is chosen so that the external scalar per edge is exactly

$$
C_h\sqrt h(1-i)\lambda_0=\lambda^{-1/2},
$$

apart from the separately audited unit-modulus quantization scalar. There is one Jacobian for each integration variable, so (4.1) gives the exact product Jacobian with no additional gauge.

### 4.1 A concrete trace-norm criterion

Let

$$
A=1-\partial_y^2+y^2.
$$

Its eigenvalues are `2(n+1)`, so

$$
\|A^{-1}\|_2^2=\frac{\pi^2}{24}.
$$

For a sufficiently regular kernel `k`, the operator `S` with kernel `A_{y'}A_y k` satisfies `K=A^{-1}SA^{-1}`. Thus

$$
\|K\|_1
\le\frac{\pi^2}{24}\,
\|A_{y'}A_y k\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^2)}.
\tag{4.2}
$$

This reduces every trace-norm assertion used here to a finite number of weighted kernel derivative estimates.

For each fixed nonzero small `s`, (2.5), spatial analyticity of `F` and `B_h`, and smoothness of `psi` show that (4.1) and all needed derivatives decrease faster than any power. In particular, (4.2) proves that every `K_s(x)` is trace class.

### 4.2 Uniform convergence to a variable Gaussian

On the straight portion of the contour, `z_s(y)=csy`. Write `H_x=D^2F(Q,q)`. Then

$$
k_0(x;y',y)
=\lambda_0^{-1}B_0(Q,q)
\exp\bigl((y',y)H_x(y',y)^T\bigr).
\tag{4.3}
$$

Here `ic^2=2`. The real symmetric matrix `H_x` is uniformly negative definite.

Taylor expansion on a smaller straight portion, followed by (4.2), proves convergence to (4.3) uniformly in the base point. The nonstraight part causes no difficulty: if one of `|sy|`, `|sy'|` is bounded below by a fixed positive number, (2.5) supplies `exp(-c_0/s^2)` in that variable. Any fixed power of `s^{-1}`, any polynomial weight in the coordinates, and the finitely many derivatives in (4.2) are absorbed by this decay. Those tails are therefore smaller than every fixed power of `s` in the weighted norm of (4.2).

It is not necessary, and would be inaccurate, to claim a Gaussian bound in `y` throughout the saturated tails. The correct local Gaussian bound and tail estimate (2.6) suffice.

### 4.3 Uniform numerical neighborhood of the fixed Gaussian

The reference operator is

$$
k_G(y',y)
=\lambda_0^{-1}
\exp\left[-\frac34(y'^2+y^2)+\frac12y'y\right].
\tag{4.4}
$$

The normalized ground state is

$$
e_0(y)=\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}
e^{-y^2/\sqrt2}.
$$

The Hermite generating function, equivalently the Mehler identity, gives the eigenvalues

$$
1,\rho,\rho^2,\ldots.
$$

Let `P=e_0 e_0^*`. Consequently,

$$
\|K_G-P\|=\rho,
\qquad
\|K_G-P\|_1=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}
=\frac{\sqrt2-1}{2}<0.207107.
\tag{4.5}
$$

A very coarse explicit estimate already makes the perturbation negligible. If `eta<=0.01`, every matrix on the segment from `H_0` to `H_x` has entries of absolute value at most one and is at most `-I/4`. Also,

$$
|B_0-1|\le3\eta.
$$

Differentiating a Gaussian along this matrix segment gives a linear combination, with total coefficient at most `4 eta`, of the kernels `y_i y_j exp(y^THy)`. To check an explicit constant in (4.2), for `p=1` or any coordinate monomial of degree two one may use

$$
|A_{y'}A_y(p\,e^{Y^THY})|
\le8\cdot10^5(1+|Y|)^6e^{-|Y|^2/4}.
$$

This follows by differentiating the Gaussian at most four times, using `|H_{ij}|<=1`, and applying Leibniz's rule. The `L^2` norm of `(1+|Y|)^6 exp(-|Y|^2/4)` in two dimensions is below `2.5*10^4`; expand `(1+r)^12 <= 2^11(1+r^12)` and evaluate the two radial Gaussian moments. Thus (4.2), including the amplitude change, gives the deliberately generous bound

$$
\sup_x\|K_0(x)-K_G\|_1\le10^{12}\eta
\le2\cdot10^{19}|t|<10^{-60}.
\tag{4.6}
$$

Together with uniform convergence to `K_0`, this proves that there is one `h_0>0`, independent of `x` and of the iterate length, for which

$$
\sup_x\|K_{\sqrt h}(x)-K_G\|_1\le10^{-4},
\qquad 0<h<h_0.
\tag{4.7}
$$

An explicit numerical value of `h_0` is unnecessary for an assertion about all sufficiently small `h`.

### 4.4 Product traces

The kernels are smooth with rapidly decreasing tails, and cyclic products are absolutely integrable by (2.5). The trace of a cyclic product is its cyclic kernel integral. For example, this follows by expanding the smooth rapidly decreasing kernels in the Hermite basis, where the coefficients decrease sufficiently fast to justify the trace and all integrals, or by applying finite Hermite projections and passing to the limit.

Combining (1.6), the contour identity, and the normalization in (4.1), the given sector integral is therefore exactly

$$
\zeta^m\omega_x(h)\,\lambda^{-m/2}
\operatorname{Tr}
\left[K_h(\Psi^{m-1}x)\cdots K_h(x)\right],
\tag{4.8}
$$

where from now on `K_h` abbreviates `K_{sqrt(h)}`, and the audited outside factors have unit modulus. The full finite quantum identity still relies on the separate exact sector input stated at the beginning.

## 5. A global, boundary-independent rank-one splitting

This part uses only (4.7) and the invertibility of the base map. Set

$$
\varepsilon=10^{-4},\qquad r=2.5\cdot10^{-4},\qquad
\delta=\varepsilon(1+r)=0.000100025.
$$

Use the fixed decomposition `H=C e_0 direct-sum H_perp`. For `w in H_perp`, `||w||<=r`, define

$$
g_x(w)=
\frac{(I-P)K_h(x)(e_0+w)}
{e_0^*K_h(x)(e_0+w)}.
\tag{5.1}
$$

The denominator has modulus at least `1-delta`, and

$$
\|g_x(w)\|
\le\frac{\rho r+\varepsilon(1+r)}{1-\delta}
<0.000143<r.
$$

Its Lipschitz constant on this ball is at most

$$
\frac{\rho+\varepsilon(1+r)}{1-\delta}<0.172.
\tag{5.2}
$$

On the Banach space of bounded continuous sections set

$$
(\mathcal G w)(x)
=g_{\Psi^{-1}x}(w(\Psi^{-1}x)).
$$

This is a strict contraction. Its unique fixed point gives

$$
v_h(x)=e_0+w_h(x),\qquad e_0^*v_h(x)=1,
$$

and

$$
K_h(x)v_h(x)=\gamma_h(x)v_h(\Psi x),
\qquad
\gamma_h(x)=e_0^*K_h(x)v_h(x).
\tag{5.3}
$$

The construction uses the entire past orbit through the uniform contraction limit, so it has no dependence on a chosen finite boundary condition. It gives a globally normalized section because (1.4) provides one fixed Hilbert-space trivialization. In particular,

$$
|\gamma_h(x)-1|\le\delta<0.0002.
\tag{5.4}
$$

For the dual graph, use the future recurrence on complex-linear functionals:

$$
u_h(x)=
\frac{u_h(\Psi x)K_h(x)}
{u_h(\Psi x)K_h(x)e_0},
\qquad u_h(x)e_0=1.
\tag{5.5}
$$

The same bounds give a unique `u_h=e_0^*+z_h`, with `||z_h||<=r` and `z_h e_0=0`. Define

$$
c_h(x)=u_h(x)v_h(x),\qquad
\ell_h(x)=u_h(x)/c_h(x).
$$

Then `|c_h-1|<=r^2`, so this normalization is valid globally, and

$$
\ell_h(x)v_h(x)=1,
\qquad
\ell_h(\Psi x)K_h(x)=\gamma_h(x)\ell_h(x).
\tag{5.6}
$$

For the second equality, evaluate (5.5) on `v_h(x)` and use (5.3). Thus the right and left normalizations are compatible; no independently chosen orbit-dependent scalar is being inserted.

Put

$$
R_h(x)=\gamma_h(x)v_h(\Psi x)\ell_h(x),
\qquad
\mathcal B_h(x)=K_h(x)-R_h(x).
\tag{5.7}
$$

Equations (5.3) and (5.6) imply

$$
\mathcal B_h(x)v_h(x)=0,
\qquad
\ell_h(\Psi x)\mathcal B_h(x)=0.
\tag{5.8}
$$

Hence, on consecutive edges,

$$
R_h(\Psi x)\mathcal B_h(x)=0,
\qquad
\mathcal B_h(\Psi x)R_h(x)=0.
\tag{5.9}
$$

All mixed words vanish exactly.

For completeness, the numerical residual bounds do not require sharp graph estimates. From the above normalizations,

$$
\|v_h(\Psi x)\ell_h(x)-P\|_1
\le\frac{2r}{1-r},
\qquad
\|v_h(\Psi x)\ell_h(x)\|_1
\le\frac{1+r}{1-r}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\|R_h(x)-P\|_1
\le\delta\frac{1+r}{1-r}+\frac{2r}{1-r}
<0.000601.
$$

Using (4.5) and (4.7) gives

$$
\|\mathcal B_h(x)\|
<0.172274<0.174,
\qquad
\|\mathcal B_h(x)\|_1
<0.207808<0.209.
\tag{5.10}
$$

For a periodic base point of period dividing `m`, let

$$
\Gamma_h(x,m)=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\gamma_h(\Psi^j x).
$$

Multiplying (5.7) and using (5.9) yields

$$
\operatorname{Tr}\prod_{j=m-1}^{0}K_h(\Psi^j x)
=\Gamma_h(x,m)
+\operatorname{Tr}\prod_{j=m-1}^{0}\mathcal B_h(\Psi^j x).
\tag{5.11}
$$

The rank-one trace is precisely `Gamma_h`, since the base point is periodic and `ell_h(x)v_h(x)=1`.

## 6. The summed error is small for every iterate

From (5.10), including `m=1`,

$$
\left|\operatorname{Tr}\prod_{j=m-1}^{0}\mathcal B_h(\Psi^j x)\right|
\le0.209(0.174)^{m-1}.
\tag{6.1}
$$

Suppose, as in the separate classical argument, the number of fixed points is less than `lambda^m`. The outside phase in (4.8) has modulus one. The total error after inserting (5.11) in the exact sector sum is then bounded by

$$
\lambda^{m/2}\,0.209(0.174)^{m-1}
=\frac{0.209}{0.174}
\left(0.174\sqrt\lambda\right)^m.
$$

Numerically,

$$
\frac{0.209}{0.174}<1.202<2,
\qquad
0.174\sqrt\lambda
=0.174(1+\sqrt2)<0.421.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\quad |\text{summed residual at time }m|
<2(0.421)^m,\qquad m=1,2,\ldots.\quad}
\tag{6.2}
$$

The small-h threshold is the single edge threshold in (4.7), independent of `m`. No logarithmic restriction on `m` is used. The assertion for the actual finite quantum trace is as strong as, and only as strong as, the exact sector input.

## 7. The correct small-s regularity statement

There is a useful distinction between an operator Taylor expansion and differentiability in `h`. The complete transverse operator generally has odd powers of `s=sqrt(h)`, so it need not be differentiable in `h` at zero.

Also, `B_h` being uniformly `C^2` in `h` does not, by itself, make `B_{s^2}` literally `C^4` throughout a nonzero `s` interval: four `s` derivatives away from zero can involve the third and fourth `h` derivatives. That stronger claim is unnecessary here.

The sufficient, valid statement is

$$
K_s(x)=K_0(x)+sK_1(x)+s^2K_2(x)+s^3K_3(x)
+O_{\mathcal S_1}(s^4),
\tag{7.1}
$$

uniformly in the base point. The family is also `C^2` in `s` near zero.

To prove (7.1), first use the uniform amplitude expansion

$$
B_{s^2}(Z)=B_0(Z)+s^2\partial_hB_0(Z)+O(s^4)
$$

in a smaller spatial strip. Expand `B_0` spatially through degree three and `partial_h B_0` through degree one. Expand the centered phase through degree five before division by `s^2`, then expand its exponential through degree three. On the straight portion, the real-part estimate remains a uniform negative quadratic form for every intermediate Taylor parameter. The remainders are bounded by `s^4` times a fixed polynomial in the coordinates times a decaying Gaussian. The same statement holds for the spatial derivatives and weights in (4.2). On the nonstraight part, the previously proved `exp(-c_0/s^2)` estimate is smaller than every required power. This proves (7.1) in trace norm.

The graph construction inherits (7.1). One direct proof avoids any unproved infinite expansion. Expand the contraction equation for the right graph through degree three. At each positive order the unknown coefficient is obtained by inverting `I-T`, where `T` is the derivative of the zeroth-order graph transform at its fixed section and `||T||<0.172`. Build the resulting polynomial approximate section. Its defect in the full contraction equation is uniformly `O(s^4)`. The contraction estimate bounds the difference from the true fixed section by the defect divided by `1-0.172`. The scalar `gamma_s`, the dual section, and the residual have the corresponding finite Taylor expansions.

## 8. Reflection parity and the pointwise first-order modulus

Extend (4.1) algebraically to negative real `s`, keeping `h=s^2` in the amplitude. Let `(Rf)(y)=f(-y)`. Since `psi` is odd and `psi'` is even,

$$
K_{-s}(x)=R K_s(x)R,
\qquad Re_0=e_0.
\tag{8.1}
$$

The uniqueness of the normalized graph section gives

$$
v_{-s}(x)=Rv_s(x),
\qquad
\gamma_{-s}(x)=\gamma_s(x).
\tag{8.2}
$$

The dual graph gives the analogous identities for `ell_s` and `mathcal B_s`. Thus the scalar is exactly even in `s`. This is stronger than saying only that a periodic trace is even.

A finite-jet reality argument makes the first h coefficient pointwise imaginary. On the straight local contour write the real homogeneous Taylor polynomials of the centered phase as `F_{x,n}`. Then

$$
\frac{i\widetilde F_x(csY)}{s^2}
=2\sum_{k=0}^{3}c^ks^kF_{x,k+2}(Y)
+O(s^4|Y|^6),
\tag{8.3}
$$

because `ic^(k+2)=2c^k`. The amplitude input is

$$
B_h=B_0+ihb_1+O(h^2),\qquad B_0,b_1\text{ real},
$$

and `i=-c^2/2`. Its spatial expansion and (8.3) show that the coefficients in (7.1) have the structure

$$
K_j=c^jA_j,\qquad 0\le j\le3,
\tag{8.4}
$$

where every `A_j` is a real operator. Saturated tails do not change any of these finite Taylor coefficients.

The zeroth-order graph is real. Its recursive coefficient equations use only multiplication, division by a nonzero scalar, the complex-linear functional `e_0^*`, and the real inverse `I-T`. They use no conjugation of the varying section. Induction therefore gives the same factor `c^j` for the coefficients of `v_s` and `gamma_s`. Combined with evenness, this proves

$$
\gamma_s(x)=\gamma_0(x)+c^2s^2g_2(x)+O(s^4),
\qquad g_2(x)\in\mathbb R.
\tag{8.5}
$$

Since `c^2=-2i`, in terms of positive `h` this reads

$$
\gamma_h(x)=\gamma_0(x)+h\gamma_1(x)+O(h^2),
\qquad
\gamma_0(x)>0,\quad \gamma_1(x)\in i\mathbb R.
\tag{8.6}
$$

All bounds are uniform in the base point. Positivity follows from reality at zero and `|gamma_0-1|<0.0002`. As `gamma_0` stays away from zero,

$$
\log|\gamma_h(x)|^2
=2\log\gamma_0(x)+O(h^2)
\tag{8.7}
$$

uniformly, pointwise in `x`. No periodic-repeat argument is needed for the cancellation of the first-order real part once this real finite-jet structure is established.

## 9. The exact zeroth-order cycle normalization

Let `x` be periodic of period `m`, and let `Lambda_m>1` be the unstable multiplier of `D Psi^m(x)`. In the near-cat situation the multipliers are positive and are `Lambda_m` and `Lambda_m^{-1}`.

Here is the Gaussian normalization explicitly. Put

$$
b_j=F_{Qq}(q_{j+1},q_j)>0,
$$

and let `mathcal H_m` be the Hessian of the cyclic action (1.5) in the `m` position variables. It is negative definite. Direct integration of (4.3) gives

$$
\operatorname{Tr}\prod_{j=m-1}^{0}K_0(x_j)
=\lambda_0^{-m}\left(\prod_j2\sqrt{b_j}\right)
\frac{\pi^{m/2}}{\sqrt{\det(-\mathcal H_m)}}.
\tag{9.1}
$$

The elementary discrete Hill identity is

$$
\det(-\mathcal H_m)
=\left(\prod_j b_j\right)
\bigl|\det(I-D\Psi^m(x))\bigr|.
\tag{9.2}
$$

To verify its normalization, the Jacobi recurrence for the second variation is

$$
b_{j-1}u_{j-1}+d_ju_j+b_ju_{j+1}=0,
\qquad
d_j=F_{qq}(x_j)+F_{QQ}(x_{j-1}).
$$

Its transfer matrix product is conjugate to `D Psi^m`. Impose twisted boundary data `u_{j+m}=z u_j`. The determinant of the twisted cyclic Hessian has the two extreme terms

$$
(-1)^{m-1}\left(\prod_jb_j\right)(z+z^{-1}).
$$

Its zeros are the eigenvalues of that transfer product, so the determinant equals

$$
(-1)^{m-1}\left(\prod_jb_j\right)
\left(z+z^{-1}-\operatorname{tr}(D\Psi^m)\right).
$$

At `z=1`, using the negative definiteness of the Hessian, this is (9.2). For `m=1,2`, the same formula holds when the coincident cyclic edges are counted with their actual multiplicities; direct substitution in the one- and two-variable Hessians gives the same identity.

Since `lambda_0=2 sqrt(pi)/sqrt(lambda)`, equations (9.1) and (9.2) yield

$$
\lambda^{-m/2}
\operatorname{Tr}\prod_{j=m-1}^{0}K_0(x_j)
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\det(I-D\Psi^m(x))|}}.
\tag{9.3}
$$

Repeat this fixed orbit `r` times. Let `Gamma_0=Gamma_0(x,m)`. Equations (5.11) and (9.3) imply

$$
\lambda^{-rm/2}
\left(\Gamma_0^r+R_r(0)\right)
=\frac{\Lambda_m^{-r/2}}{1-\Lambda_m^{-r}},
\tag{9.4}
$$

where

$$
|R_r(0)|\le0.209(0.174)^{rm-1},
\qquad
\Gamma_0\ge(1-0.0002)^m>0.
$$

Therefore `R_r(0)/Gamma_0^r -> 0` exponentially. Taking positive r-th roots in (9.4) gives

$$
\boxed{\quad
\lambda^{-m}\Gamma_0(x,m)^2\Lambda_m=1.
\quad}
\tag{9.5}
$$

This repetition argument takes a limit only of exact Gaussian formulas. It uses no stationary-phase statement uniform in the number of repetitions.

Combining (8.7) and (9.5) proves

$$
\boxed{\quad
\log\left(
\lambda^{-m}|\Gamma_h(x,m)|^2\Lambda_m
\right)=O(mh^2),
\quad}
\tag{9.6}
$$

with one constant uniform over all periodic points and all positive periods. Equivalently, the quantity inside the logarithm lies between `exp(-Cmh^2)` and `exp(Cmh^2)`.

## 10. Independent check by differentiating repeated residual traces

There is also a valid repetition proof of the cancellation needed in (9.6). It is useful if one chooses to use only stationary-phase reality rather than the pointwise finite-jet argument in Section 8.

Fix a periodic orbit of length `m`, let `L=rm`, and put

$$
R_L(s)=\operatorname{Tr}
\prod_{j=L-1}^{0}\mathcal B_s(x_{j\bmod m}).
$$

The reflection identity implies that `R_L` is even. Uniform `C_s^2` trace-norm regularity gives finite constants

$$
M_k=\sup_x
\left\|\partial_s^k\mathcal B_s(x)|_{s=0}\right\|_1,
\qquad k=1,2.
$$

Differentiate the product twice. A term with one second derivative is bounded by `M_2 b^(L-1)`, and a term with two first derivatives is bounded by `M_1^2 b^(L-2)`, where `b=0.174`. One differentiated factor supplies the trace norm, the other factors supply operator norms. Thus, for `L>=2`,

$$
|R_L''(0)|
\le L M_2 b^{L-1}
+L(L-1)M_1^2b^{L-2}.
\tag{10.1}
$$

For `L=1` only the first term is present. No inverse cocycle or inverse residual operator appears in this estimate.

Write the even scalar expansion as

$$
\gamma_s(x)=\gamma_0(x)+s^2\gamma_1(x)+O(s^4).
$$

For the fixed period,

$$
\Gamma_s(x,m)=\Gamma_0
\left(1+s^2 a+O_m(s^4)\right),
\qquad
a=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{\gamma_1(x_j)}{\gamma_0(x_j)}.
$$

Its r-th power has h-linear coefficient `r Gamma_0^r a`. Fixed-dimensional stationary phase, with real phase, real principal amplitude, and imaginary first amplitude coefficient, says that the normalized sector has real positive leading coefficient and zero real part of its h-linear coefficient. Applying this separately at each fixed `L=rm`, and using the exact cocycle decomposition, gives

$$
\operatorname{Re}\left(
r\Gamma_0^r a+\frac12R_L''(0)
\right)=0.
$$

The scalar factor `lambda^{-L/2}` is real and nonzero and cancels from this equation. Since

$$
\frac{b^L}{\Gamma_0^r}
\le\left(\frac{b}{1-0.0002}\right)^L,
$$

equation (10.1), divided by `r Gamma_0^r`, tends to zero exponentially as `r -> infinity`. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Re}
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{\gamma_1(x_j)}{\gamma_0(x_j)}=0.
\tag{10.2}
$$

Together with the uniform `O(h^2)` scalar remainder and (9.5), this independently proves (9.6). No uniform-in-`L` stationary-phase error is used: only an exact identity for the first coefficient at each fixed finite `L`, followed by an explicit residual bound.

## 11. Audit conclusions and dependency boundaries

The bounded contour tails are compatible with trace class and with exact Gaussian-regularized Fresnel deformation. The useful estimate is monotone-contour coercivity, not an invalid global Gaussian estimate in the rescaled coordinate.

The exact deck law removes the centered-kernel gauge. The invertibility of `Psi` and the fixed even reference state provide globally normalized, boundary-independent right and left graph sections. Their normalization produces exact vanishing of every mixed product and the exact periodic product-trace formula.

The remainder bound `2(0.421)^m` is valid for every positive integer `m`, at one sufficiently small h threshold, once the exact quantum sector identity and the fixed-point count are supplied. There is no local analytic reason to restrict it to logarithmic times.

The scalar is even by the unique normalized graph construction. The substantive remaining dependency for an all-time theorem about the actual finite quantum operator is the exact global sector identification, including its odd-lattice scalar. That identity is logically separate from all the estimates proved in this note.
