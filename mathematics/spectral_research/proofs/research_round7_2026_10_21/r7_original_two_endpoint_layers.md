# Coordinate visibility for the original Jacobi family, and the remaining interior obstruction

Research checkpoint, 21 October 2026. This note keeps the exact matrices in
the question and the fixed parameter $\tau=10^{-80}$. It proves
additional finite-dimensional statements for an infinite class of the
original dimensions. It does not establish any estimate in
$H_{\rm diag}$.

## 1. What was already proved and what is added here

The frozen source

`research_round2_2026_10_20/arithmetic_padic_jacobi_simplicity.md`

has SHA-256

`f311596933d383976547ac5b2cd633b91107074da0fcab58db989eedcc4568df`.

For every odd $N$ with $7\nmid N$, it proves simplicity of $A_N$,
nonvanishing of every square minor of $M_N$ in an orthonormal
$A_N$-eigenbasis, and simplicity of
$U_N(z)=e^{izNA_N}M_N$ for each nonzero algebraic $z$. The separate
source `arithmetic_endpoint_cyclicity.md` proves that the two Jacobi
endpoint coordinates are cyclic and observable. Those are previous
results, not new conclusions of this note.

The additional result is the following.

**Theorem 1.** Let $N\ge3$ be odd, $7\nmid N$, and let
$h=(N+1)/2$. For every nonzero algebraic complex number $z$, each
coordinate vector with coordinate in

$$
\mathcal E_N=\{h-2,h-1,h,h+1\}\pmod N
\tag{1.1}
$$

is cyclic and observable for $U_N(z)$. Consequently every eigenvector
of the original $U_N=U_N(\tau)$ is nonzero at these coordinates.
Repeated elements in (1.1) are counted once. In particular, when
$N=3$, every eigenvector has full coordinate support.

The additional coordinates are $h+1$ and $h-2$, one
step into the path from its two endpoints. No estimate uniform in
$N$ on their magnitudes is asserted.

There is also an all-coordinate rank bound.

**Theorem 2.** Under the same hypotheses, set
$d(j)=\min(j,N-1-j)$ for $0\le j<N$. Both the cyclic matrix
$[e_{h+j},U_N(z)e_{h+j},\ldots,U_N(z)^{N-1}e_{h+j}]$ and its
observable counterpart have rank at least

$$
R_j=N-\max(d(j)-1,0).
\tag{1.2}
$$

For the original real parameter $\tau$, each physical coordinate is nonzero in at least
$R_j$ members of an orthonormal eigenbasis of $U_N$.
In particular, every coordinate is nonzero in at least
$(N+3)/2$ eigenvectors. Simplicity of $U_N$ in the dimensions
at issue is the already proved frozen theorem.

Theorems 1 and 2 concern the original family, not an independently
randomized phase model. They assert ranks and exact nonvanishing,
not lower bounds on the nonzero overlaps.

The same proof, using the full top-eigenspace minor property in
Section 4, gives a stronger statement for coordinate subspaces.

**Theorem 3.** Let $S\subset\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$ be nonempty,
$r=|S|$, and $d(S)=\min_{j\in S}\min(j,N-1-j)$. The
observable and cyclic matrices generated by the coordinate
subspace $\operatorname{span}\{e_{h+j}:j\in S\}$ have rank at least

$$
N-\max(d(S)-r,0).
\tag{1.3}
$$

Precisely, if $R_S$ restricts a vector to these physical
coordinates, the observable matrix stacks the rows of
$R_SU_N(z)^m$, $0\le m<N$. The cyclic matrix concatenates
the columns of $U_N(z)^mR_S^*$ for the same range of $m$.

In particular, if $r\ge d(S)$, this subspace is fully cyclic
and observable for $U_N(z)$. Every coordinate set of size

$$
r\ge r_0(N):=\left\lceil\frac{N-1}{3}\right\rceil
\tag{1.4}
$$

has this property. Therefore every eigenvector of the original
matrix is supported on at least

$$
\boxed{N-\left\lceil\frac{N-1}{3}\right\rceil+1}
\tag{1.5}
$$

physical coordinates. This concerns exact zeros; it does not
give a mass lower bound on any of these coordinates. As with the
other theorems, the dimension hypothesis remains $7\nmid N$.

## 2. The exact path and the two valuation estimates being used

Put $\omega=(-3+4i)/5$, order the coordinates by
$x_j=h+j\pmod N$, and let $P$ be this permutation. With
$D=\operatorname{diag}(1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{N-1})$, the matrix

$$
L=200D^{-1}P^*A_NPD
\tag{2.1}
$$

is real symmetric tridiagonal. Its diagonal is
$d_j=200V(x_j/N)$, and its internal off-diagonal entries are
$b_j=r((j+1)/N)>0$. The closing edge vanishes. The modular identity
$x_j+h=j+1\pmod N$ is the reason for this precise argument of $r$.
All statements here include $N=3$; the shifts $+1,-1$ remain
distinct modulo an odd $N\ge3$.

At either place above 5 specified by $1+2i$ or $1-2i$, normalized
so that $v(5)=1$, one has

$$
v(d_j)\ge0,\qquad 0\le v(b_j)\le\tfrac12.
\tag{2.2}
$$

At the first place, denoted $v_+$, $v_+(\omega)=1$; at the second,
denoted $v_-$, $v_-(\omega)=-1$. The input proof verifies (2.2)
also when $5\mid N$: factor

$$
r(y/N)=-\frac{(\eta^7-1)^2(\eta+1)^2}{4\eta^8},
\qquad \eta=e_N(y),
$$

and use $v(1-\eta)=1/(4\cdot5^{a-1})$ when the surviving root has
order $5^a$. An odd-order root has $1+\eta$ a unit.

Choose real eigenvectors of $L$, and let $\Phi$ be their matrix.
For any $k$-element eigenvalue set $E$, endpoint minors are nonzero,
and, writing $J_k^+=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}$ and
$J_k^-=\{N-k,\ldots,N-1\}$, one has at either place

$$
v\left(\frac{\det\Phi_{J,E}}{\det\Phi_{J_k^+,E}}\right)
\ge-\frac12\left(\sum J-\sum J_k^+\right),
\tag{2.3}
$$

$$
v\left(\frac{\det\Phi_{J,E}}{\det\Phi_{J_k^-,E}}\right)
\ge-\frac12\left(\sum J_k^--\sum J\right).
\tag{2.4}
$$

These inequalities follow from the monic Jacobi recurrence
$p_{j+1}=(X-d_j)p_j-b_{j-1}^2p_{j-1}$, division of its alternant by
the eigenvalue Vandermonde, and the same recurrence from the opposite
endpoint. The quotients are valuation-integral polynomials divided by
the indicated products of edges. They do not presume that eigenvalue
differences are units.

Let $M_0=P^*M_NP$. Its entries are Fourier entries times nonzero
root-of-unity row and column factors and $N^{-1/2}$. Whenever
$I_0,J_0$ are consecutive coordinate sets of the same size, their
Fourier minor is nonzero and minimizes the valuation among all minors
of that size:

$$
v\left(\frac{\det(M_0)_{I,J}}{\det(M_0)_{I_0,J_0}}\right)\ge0.
\tag{2.5}
$$

For completeness, the minimization uses the factorization of a
Fourier alternant as a Vandermonde times an integral Schur polynomial.
At a place above 5, the valuation of the Vandermonde is a positive
linear combination of residue-pair counts
$\sum_r\binom{\#\{i\in I:i\equiv r\pmod d\}}2$, for the relevant
divisors $d$ of $N$. Consecutive sets balance each count and
minimize all of them simultaneously. This is the reason (2.5) holds
for composite $N$, including $5\mid N$.

## 3. A bordered minor for the two additional coordinates

Define the unnormalized overlap matrix

$$
C=\Phi^T D^{-1}M_0D\Phi.
$$

Fix $|E|=k$, $|F|=k-1$, and a physical path index $j$. Append
the row $\omega^j\Phi_{j,E}$ below $C_{F,E}$, and denote the
resulting determinant by $H_{j;F,E}$.

Expanding the appended row after two applications of Cauchy--Binet
gives the exact identity

$$
H_{j;F,E}=\sum_{\substack{|I|=|J|=k-1\\j\notin J}}
\epsilon(J,j)\det\Phi_{I,F}\det(M_0)_{I,J}
\det\Phi_{J\cup\{j\},E}
\omega^{j+\sum J-\sum I},
\tag{3.1}
$$

where $\epsilon(J,j)\in\{1,-1\}$ is the sign of placing the
appended coordinate into increasing order. Unlike at an endpoint,
this sign need not be constant. Its valuation is zero, which is all
that will be used.

### 3.1. Indices contained in the first $k$ coordinates

Suppose $0\le j<k$. At $v_+$ the unique smallest exponent of
$\omega$ in (3.1) occurs at

$$
I_0=\{N-k+1,\ldots,N-1\},\qquad
J_0=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\setminus\{j\}.
\tag{3.2}
$$

The set $J_0$ need not be consecutive, so the
old minimum-valuation assertion (2.5) does not apply directly to
this extreme Fourier minor. The missing-column calculation in
Section 7 proves instead that its valuation exceeds the minimum by
an amount $\delta$ satisfying $0\le\delta\le1/4$.

For a different nonzero summand of (3.1), put

$$
g_R=\sum I_0-\sum I,\qquad g_C=\sum J-\sum J_0.
$$

Both numbers are nonnegative integers, and $g_R+g_C\ge1$.
The two Jacobi minor ratios have valuations at least
$-g_R/2$ and $-g_C/2$, respectively, because
$J_0\cup\{j\}=J_k^+$. The Fourier-minor ratio has valuation at
least $-\delta$. The phase ratio contributes $g_R+g_C$.
Therefore the ratio of this summand to the extreme summand has
valuation at least

$$
\frac{g_R+g_C}{2}-\delta\ge\frac14>0.
\tag{3.4}
$$

The extreme term is nonzero and uniquely dominates. Hence
$H_{j;F,E}\ne0$ whenever $j<k$, for all $E,F$ of the
stated sizes. For $k=1,j=0$, the formula simply reduces to
the nonzero endpoint entry of one Jacobi eigenvector, and no
Fourier minor of positive size is needed.

### 3.2. Indices contained in the final $k$ coordinates

Suppose $j\ge N-k$. At $v_-$ the corresponding extreme pair is

$$
I_0=\{0,\ldots,k-2\},\qquad
J_0=\{N-k,\ldots,N-1\}\setminus\{j\}.
\tag{3.3}
$$

Again the Jacobi minor uses exactly the final $k$ coordinates.
Reflecting the column indices reduces this to deletion of one
column from the initial $k$ columns, up to roots of unity.
Section 7 bounds its excess valuation by $1/4$.

For a competing pair put
$g_R=\sum I-\sum I_0$ and
$g_C=\sum J_0-\sum J$. Because $v_-(\omega)=-1$, the same
calculation (3.4) applies. It proves
$H_{j;F,E}\ne0$ whenever $j\ge N-k$.
In terms of the distance $d(j)=\min(j,N-1-j)$, the two parts
prove

$$
H_{j;F,E}\ne0\qquad\text{for every }k\ge d(j)+1.
\tag{3.5}
$$

## 4. Every coordinate minor of a top Jacobi eigenspace is nonzero

The largest eigenvalue of a real symmetric Jacobi matrix with
positive internal edges has an eigenvector with all entries
strictly positive. Here is an elementary proof sufficient for the
application. A maximizer of the Rayleigh quotient can be replaced by
the absolute values of its entries without decreasing the quotient,
because all off-diagonal entries are positive. If a nonnegative
maximizing eigenvector had a zero entry, its eigenvalue equation at
that entry would force its adjacent entries to be zero. Connectedness
would then force the whole vector to vanish. Thus all its entries
are positive. Simplicity also follows from the endpoint recurrence.

Accordingly, the coordinate coefficient for the leading
large-real-time eigenvalue branch is nonzero at every physical
coordinate. The unresolved issue for an interior coordinate is in
the branches $k\ge2$, not in this first branch.

For the coordinate-subspace assertion we need the corresponding
fact in every exterior power. For $1\le k\le N$, the additive
compound of $L$ on $\bigwedge^k\mathbb R^N$ is

$$
L^{[k]}(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k)
=\sum_{a=1}^k v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge Lv_a\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k.
\tag{4.1}
$$

In the ordered coordinate wedge basis indexed by increasing
$k$-subsets, its off-diagonal entries are nonnegative. A
nonzero one moves a single index by one to an unoccupied adjacent
site; no other index is passed, so the wedge sign is positive.
Its coefficient is the positive Jacobi edge at that site. The
graph of these moves is connected: any $k$-subset can be
moved to $\{0,\ldots,k-1\}$ by moving its entries left in
increasing order as vacancies become available.

The absolute-value Rayleigh-quotient argument above therefore
applies to $L^{[k]}$ as well. Its largest-eigenvalue eigenvector
has all coordinate entries strictly positive, up to an overall
sign. The eigenvalues of this symmetric compound are sums of
$k$ distinct eigenvalues of $L$; this follows by applying
(4.1) to wedges of an orthonormal eigenbasis. Its unique largest
eigenvalue is the sum of the $k$ largest eigenvalues. The
corresponding wedge has coordinates

$$
\det\Phi_{I,E_k},\qquad |I|=k,
$$

where $E_k$ denotes those $k$ eigenvectors. It follows that

$$
\boxed{\det\Phi_{I,E_k}\ne0\quad\text{for every }|I|=k.}
\tag{4.2}
$$

Multiplication by the nonzero diagonal gauge factors preserves
nonvanishing of every such minor. Consequently a nonzero vector
in the top $k$ eigenspace of the physical $A_N$ cannot vanish
on any $k$ coordinates: the corresponding square restriction
matrix is invertible by (4.2).

## 5. Why a successfully proved bordered-minor statement transfers to the exact parameter

Order the eigenvalues of $A_N$ as
$a_1>\cdots>a_N$, take an orthonormal eigenbasis $Q$, and put
$B=Q^*M_NQ$. Write $b_k=\det B_{[1:k],[1:k]}$, with $b_0=1$.
All these determinants are nonzero by the frozen theorem. For large
positive real $t$, the eigenvalues of
$\operatorname{diag}(e^{ta_i})B$ have the distinct scales

$$
\lambda_k(t)e^{-ta_k}\longrightarrow b_k/b_{k-1}.
\tag{5.1}
$$

This follows by comparing the two dominant terms of the
characteristic polynomial on the scale $X=e^{ta_k}Y$; the other
terms are exponentially smaller. Rouché's theorem yields one simple
root near the nonzero limit for each $k$.

With $I=\{1,\ldots,k-1\}$, a right eigenvector normalized to have
its $k$-th entry 1 tends to

$$
r_k^{(0)}=(-B_{I,I}^{-1}B_{I,k},1,0,\ldots,0).
\tag{5.2}
$$

Here is a direct justification of this normalization and limit.
Let $T=\{k+1,\ldots,N\}$. For an arbitrary eigenvector
$r=(r_I,r_k,r_T)$, the rows in $I$ give
$$
\bigl(B_{I,I}-\lambda_k(t)
       \operatorname{diag}(e^{-ta_i})_{i\in I}\bigr)r_I
=-B_{I,k}r_k-B_{I,T}r_T.
$$
Its coefficient matrix tends to the invertible $B_{I,I}$,
so $\|r_I\|\le C(|r_k|+\|r_T\|)$. The rows in $T$,
after division by $\lambda_k(t)$, give
$\|r_T\|\le\epsilon_t(|r_k|+\|r_I\|)$, with
$\epsilon_t\to0$, because
$e^{ta_i}/\lambda_k(t)\to0$ for $i>k$.
If $r_k=0$, these two bounds force $r_I=r_T=0$
for large $t$, a contradiction. After setting $r_k=1$
they give bounded $r_I$, $r_T\to0$, and the first
displayed equation yields exactly (5.2). Empty head or tail
sets require no change.

For the physical coordinate row $c_j=e_{x_j}^*Q$, the limiting
coordinate is therefore

$$
c_jr_k^{(0)}=
\frac1{b_{k-1}}
\det\begin{pmatrix}B_{I,[1:k]}\\(c_j)_{[1:k]}\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{5.3}
$$

To connect (5.3) with (3.1), write
$Q=PD\Phi R$, where $R$ is the nonzero diagonal
normalization. The numerator of (5.3) is
$H_{j;I,[1:k]}$ multiplied by
$\prod_{a\in I}R_{aa}\prod_{a=1}^kR_{aa}$.
All these factors are nonzero, so the bordered nonvanishing
proved in Section 3 applies without a change of normalization.

For $j=1,N-2$, all these quantities are nonzero: Section 3 covers
$k\ge2$, and Section 4 covers $k=1$. The observability
determinant is nonzero for large $t$, by the eigenvalue
Vandermonde. It is therefore a nonzero finite exponential polynomial
$\sum_\nu q_\nu e^{izN\beta_\nu}$, with algebraic coefficients
and algebraic frequencies, after equal frequencies are collected.
Lindemann--Weierstrass implies nonvanishing at every nonzero
algebraic $z$. The exact statement is Theorem 3.2 of S. A. Popescu,
*A Simple and Self-contained Proof for the Lindemann--Weierstrass
Theorem*, DOI `10.1007/978-3-031-51959-8_16`, already checked in the
frozen source. All its exponents here are distinct algebraic numbers.

Finally
$U_N(z)^*=U_N(\overline z)^{-1}$ converts observability at
$\overline z$ into cyclicity at $z$, by adjoint, multiplication by
$U_N(z)^{N-1}$, and reversal of the resulting columns.

Together with the frozen endpoint theorem, this proves Theorem 1.

To prove Theorem 2, apply (3.5) and the same large-time analysis.
When $d(j)\ge1$, the branches
$k=d(j)+1,\ldots,N$ are visible, as is the first branch by
Section 4. There are $N-d(j)+1=R_j$ such branches. When
$d(j)=0$, all $N$ branches are already visible. The
observable matrix therefore has rank at least $R_j$ for all
sufficiently large positive $t$. One of its finitely many
$R_j$-square minors is nonzero at such a point and hence is
a nonzero finite algebraic exponential polynomial. The same
Lindemann--Weierstrass argument transfers its nonvanishing to
every nonzero algebraic $z$. This proves the observable rank
bound. Adjoint and inversion give the cyclic rank bound, exactly
as above.

For a unitary matrix with simple spectrum, the rank of a
coordinate observable matrix is precisely the number of nonzero
coordinates in its eigenbasis row: multiplication by the
eigenbasis turns it into an eigenvalue Vandermonde times that
diagonal row. The distinct-node Vandermonde has full column rank
on every subset. This verifies the interpretation of (1.2)
for the original real $\tau$.

For Theorem 3, the limiting eigenvector (5.2), returned to physical
coordinates, is a nonzero vector in the top $k$ eigenspace of
$A_N$. If $k\le r=|S|$, (4.2) prevents its vanishing on all
of $S$. If $k\ge d(S)+1$, choose a coordinate in $S$
attaining $d(S)$; its limiting coefficient is nonzero by
(3.5). The union of these two ranges contains at least
$N-\max(d(S)-r,0)$ branches.

For a simple-spectrum matrix, the observable matrix of a
coordinate subspace has rank equal to the number of eigenvectors
with nonzero restriction to that subspace. This follows either
by interpolation polynomials which isolate each eigenvalue or
by applying a Vandermonde inverse to its time-indexed rows.
Thus the large-time rank has the lower bound (1.3). A nonzero
minor and Lindemann--Weierstrass transfer this bound to every
nonzero algebraic $z$, just as for Theorem 2. Adjoint and
inversion transfer it to the cyclic rank.

Finally, all members of a size-$r$ coordinate set with minimum
endpoint distance $d(S)$ lie in the interval
$\{d(S),\ldots,N-1-d(S)\}$. Therefore

$$
d(S)\le\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{2}\right\rfloor.
\tag{5.4}
$$

If $r\ge\lceil(N-1)/3\rceil$, then $N\le3r+1$, so the
right side of (5.4) is at most $r$. This proves (1.4).
An eigenvector vanishing on $r_0(N)$ coordinates would be
annihilated by every row of their full-rank observable matrix,
which is impossible. It has at most $r_0(N)-1$ zero coordinates,
which is exactly (1.5).

## 6. Boundary of the conclusion

Theorem 1 establishes four full coordinate tests for the original
matrices in every odd dimension not divisible by 7. It does not
establish full support in growing dimensions, and it does not
settle any dimension divisible by 7. The next coordinate
$j=2$ already presents a different Jacobi issue in the branch
$k=2$: its extreme coordinate minor is
$\Phi_{\{0,2\},\{1,2\}}$, rather than an endpoint minor. Its
ratio to the initial two-coordinate minor contains
$\mu_1+\mu_2-d_0-d_1$, whose 5-adic valuation is not bounded
by the estimates proved here. Its real nonvanishing alone does
not bound that valuation.

Finite cyclicity supplies no bound for the signed trace sums at
times comparable to $N$. Thus $H_{\rm diag}$, and even its
first fixed-test pair estimate in the main research ledger,
remain unproved.

## 7. Exact cost of deleting any one Fourier column

Let $q$ be a primitive $N$-th root of unity and let
$\ell=k-1$, so $1\le\ell\le N-1$. After root-of-unity
row and column factors are removed, a Fourier minor with
consecutive rows and the column set
$\{0,1,\ldots,\ell\}\setminus\{j\}$, with $0\le j\le\ell$, is

$$
\det(z_a^{b})_{0\le a<\ell,\ b\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}\setminus\{j\}},
\qquad z_a=z_0q^a.
\tag{7.1}
$$

Division by the ordinary Vandermonde gives the elementary
symmetric polynomial $e_{\ell-j}(z_0,\ldots,z_{\ell-1})$.
This identity can be seen directly by expanding
$\prod_a(X-z_a)$ and using its relation between the missing
column $z_a^j$ and the remaining columns. The elementary
symmetric polynomial on a geometric progression is, up to a
root of unity,

$$
e_{\ell-j}(z_0,\ldots,z_{\ell-1})
=u\,{\ell\brack j}_q,
\qquad
{\ell\brack j}_q
=\prod_{r=1}^{j}\frac{1-q^{\ell-j+r}}{1-q^r}.
\tag{7.2}
$$

Here $u$ is a root of unity. Formula (7.2), including the
polynomial nature of the displayed quotient, follows by
induction on $\ell$ from the elementary-symmetric recurrence
on adjoining the last variable. All its individual numerator
and denominator factors are nonzero because their indices
are between 1 and $\ell<N$.

At a place above 5, the uniform bound is

$$
\delta:=v\left({\ell\brack j}_q\right)
\quad\hbox{satisfies}\quad 0\le\delta\le\frac14.
\tag{7.3}
$$

If $5\nmid N$, every factor is a unit, so $\delta=0$.
Otherwise write $N=5^e n$, with $e\ge1$ and $(n,5)=1$.
For $1\le r<N$, the root-of-unity valuation formula gives

$$
v(1-q^r)=\sum_{s=0}^{e-1}\alpha_s\mathbf1_{n5^s\mid r},
\qquad
\alpha_0=\frac1{4\cdot5^{e-1}},\quad
\alpha_s=5^{s-e}\ (s\ge1).
\tag{7.4}
$$

Indeed the valuation is zero unless $n\mid r$. If
$r=n5^a u$, $(u,5)=1$, the sum in (7.4) is
$5^a/(4\cdot5^{e-1})$, the valuation for the root of
order $5^{e-a}$. In particular
$\sum_{s=0}^{e-1}\alpha_s=1/4$.

Apply (7.4) to the factorial quotient in (7.2). It gives the
exact identity

$$
\delta=\sum_{s=0}^{e-1}\alpha_s
\left(
\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{n5^s}\right\rfloor
-\left\lfloor\frac{j}{n5^s}\right\rfloor
-\left\lfloor\frac{\ell-j}{n5^s}\right\rfloor
\right).
\tag{7.5}
$$

Each parenthesis is 0 or 1, since $\ell=j+(\ell-j)$.
Thus (7.5) proves both bounds in (7.3), independently of
the position of the deleted column and of the powers of 5
dividing $N$. This is the strict valuation margin required
by (3.4). The same computation after reversing columns
applies at the other endpoint.

For the two-layer statement alone one only needs $j=1$,
when (7.2) reduces to the geometric sum
$(1-q^\ell)/(1-q)$. The full Gaussian-binomial valuation
bound yields Theorem 2 for every coordinate. Assuming zero
excess valuation would have been unjustified when $5\mid N$.
