# Exact 24-branch Gaussian kernel for the original $M_N^2$

21 October 2026. All identities below hold for every odd $N\ge3$, including $3\mid N$. Modular inverses always have the meaning in the question. The proof is finite and does not use a semiclassical propagation theorem.

## 1. Explicit statement

Put
$$
 a=17,\qquad b=24,\qquad c=12,\qquad a^2-bc=1,
$$
$$
 Q(x,y)=17x^2-2xy+17y^2,\qquad F=-Q,
$$
and define the finite image sum
$$
 K_N(x,y)=\frac1{\sqrt{24N}}\sum_{m=0}^{23}
       \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi i}{48N}Q(x,y+mN)\right).
 \tag{1.1}
$$
This function of integer $x,y$ is $N$-periodic in both variables. It defines a unitary $N\times N$ matrix proportional to the original $M_N^2$.

Here is an explicit phase fixing that proportionality. Let
$$
 G_N=\sum_{z=0}^{N-1}e_N(-3\,4^{-1}z^2),\qquad
 S_N=\sum_{m=0}^{23}\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi i\,17Nm^2}{48}\right),
$$
$$
 \rho_N=\sqrt{\frac N{24}}\,\frac{S_N}{G_N}.
 \tag{1.2}
$$
The denominator is nonzero, $|\rho_N|=1$, and the exact equality is
$$
 \boxed{M_N^2=\rho_N^{-1}K_N.}
 \tag{1.3}
$$
Thus, with
$$
 \Theta_m(q,r)
 =-\frac{17q^2-2qr+17r^2}{48}+\frac{mq}{24},
$$
$$
 d_{m,N}(y)
 =\frac{\rho_N^{-1}}{\sqrt{24}}
   \exp\left(2\pi i\left[-\frac{17my}{24}
                              -\frac{17Nm^2}{48}\right]\right),
 \tag{1.4}
$$
we have the exact $24$-branch formula
$$
 \boxed{
 (M_N^2)_{xy}
 =\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{m=0}^{23}
       d_{m,N}(y)e^{2\pi iN\Theta_m(x/N,y/N)},
 \qquad |d_{m,N}(y)|=\frac1{\sqrt{24}}.
 }
 \tag{1.5}
$$
The branch coefficients are $24$-periodic functions of the integer $y$.

Equivalently, all branch coefficients can be made constant by using the image phases
$$
 \widetilde\Theta_m(q,r)
 =-\frac{17q^2-2q(r+m)+17(r+m)^2}{48}.
 \tag{1.6}
$$
In this convention
$$
 (M_N^2)_{xy}
 =\frac{\rho_N^{-1}}{\sqrt{24N}}
                  \sum_{m=0}^{23}e^{2\pi iN\widetilde\Theta_m(x/N,y/N)}.
 \tag{1.7}
$$
Expansion of the square proves directly the equivalence of (1.5) and (1.7).

## 2. Periodicity of the image sum

For general $a,b,c$ with $a^2-bc=1$, the polynomial
$Q(x,Y)=ax^2-2xY+aY^2$ satisfies
$$
 Q(x,Y+bN)-Q(x,Y)
 =2bN(-x+aY)+ab^2N^2.
 \tag{2.1}
$$
After division by $2bN$, this is an integer for integer $x,Y,N$, since $ab$ is even. Therefore the summand in (1.1) is periodic in the image index $m$ with period $b=24$. Reindexing $m$ by one proves $K_N(x,y+N)=K_N(x,y)$.

The second exact polynomial identity is
$$
 Q(x+N,Y+aN)-Q(x,Y)=bc(2YN+aN^2).
 \tag{2.2}
$$
After division by $2bN$, it becomes $cY+acN/2$, again an integer because $ac=204$ is even. Thus the summand at $(x+N,m+a)$ equals the summand at $(x,m)$. Reindexing the complete set of images modulo $24$ proves $K_N(x+N,y)=K_N(x,y)$.

These are exact periodicities of the complete kernel. They do not require an individual branch in (1.5) to be periodic.

## 3. Direct Weyl covariance and the scalar identification

Write $h_N=2^{-1}\pmod N$. The original Weyl operators satisfy
$$
 T_N(u,v)T_N(r,s)
   =e_N(h_N(us-vr))T_N(u+r,v+s).
 \tag{3.1}
$$
This follows by substituting their actions on a vector and collecting the phase. The matrix
$$
 S=\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\2&3\end{pmatrix}
$$
preserves the antisymmetric form in (3.1), because $\det S=1$.

A direct expansion of the original Gaussian entries gives
$$
 M_NT_N(1,0)=T_N(3,2)M_N,\qquad
 M_NT_N(0,1)=T_N(4,3)M_N.
 \tag{3.2}
$$
For example the ratio $(M_N)_{x,y-1}/(M_N)_{xy}$ is
$$
 e_N(-4^{-1}x+3\,4^{-1}y-3\,8^{-1}),
$$
which is also the ratio produced by $T_N(3,2)M_N$. The second equality follows from the extra exponent $3x+6-8^{-1}(24x+48-8y)=y$, understood modulo $N$.

Every Weyl operator is generated by $T_N(1,0)$, $T_N(0,1)$ and a scalar phase, and (3.1) fixes those phases. Therefore (3.2) extends to
$$
 M_NT_N(u,v)=T_N(3u+4v,\,2u+3v)M_N.
$$
Squaring this equality gives
$$
 M_N^2T_N(1,0)=T_N(17,12)M_N^2,\qquad
 M_N^2T_N(0,1)=T_N(24,17)M_N^2.
 \tag{3.3}
$$

The same two covariances hold for $K_N$. The required polynomial identities are
$$
 Q(x+a,Y)-Q(x,Y-1)=bc(2x+a),
 \tag{3.4}
$$
$$
 Q(x+b,Y)-Q(x,Y)=2b\{a(x+b/2)-Y\}.
 \tag{3.5}
$$
For (3.4), the phase multiplier in $T_N(a,c)$ is exactly
$\exp(2\pi i c(x+a/2)/N)$. Indeed $ac$ is even, so the modular half $h_Nac$ and ordinary integer $ac/2$ give the same character. This multiplier cancels the extra Gaussian phase in (3.4), term by term in the images.

For (3.5), the multiplier in $T_N(b,a)$ similarly uses the ordinary integer $ab/2$, since $b$ is even. Its cancellation leaves $e_N(Y)=e_N(y)$. The periodicity from Section 2 justifies every wrapped shift. Consequently
$$
 K_NT_N(1,0)=T_N(17,12)K_N,\qquad
 K_NT_N(0,1)=T_N(24,17)K_N.
 \tag{3.6}
$$

Let $L_N=(M_N^2)^*K_N$. Unitarity of $M_N$, together with (3.3)--(3.6), shows that $L_N$ commutes with $T_N(1,0)$ and $T_N(0,1)$. Commutation with $T_N(0,1)$, whose diagonal entries are the distinct roots $e_N(x)$, forces $L_N$ to be diagonal. Commutation with the cyclic shift $T_N(1,0)$ forces all its diagonal entries to be equal. Thus
$$
 K_N=\rho_N M_N^2
 \tag{3.7}
$$
for some scalar. This is the full irreducibility argument; no metaplectic uniqueness theorem is imported.

At $x=y=0$, the original product formula gives
$$
 (M_N^2)_{00}=\frac1N\sum_{z=0}^{N-1}e_N(-3\,4^{-1}z^2)
 =G_N/N.
 \tag{3.8}
$$
Also $K_N(0,0)=S_N/\sqrt{24N}$. Sections 4 and 5 prove both values nonzero and of the same modulus. Equation (3.7) then gives exactly the scalar (1.2), including its orientation.

## 4. Exact modulus and support of the image kernel

Put $g=\gcd(N,24)$. Since $N$ is odd, $g\in\{1,3\}$. Factoring the image sum yields
$$
 K_N(x,y)=\frac{e^{-2\pi iQ(x,y)/(48N)}}{\sqrt{24N}}\,
 H(x-17y),\qquad
 H(L)=\sum_{m=0}^{23}
 e^{2\pi i\{Lm/24-17Nm^2/48\}}.
 \tag{4.1}
$$
The summand in $H$ is $24$-periodic in $m$. Expand its squared modulus and write $d=m-n$ modulo $24$:
$$
 |H(L)|^2
 =\sum_{d=0}^{23}
 e^{2\pi i\{Ld/24-17Nd^2/48\}}
       \sum_{n=0}^{23}e^{-2\pi i17Nnd/24}.
 \tag{4.2}
$$
The inner sum is $24$ exactly when $24\mid17Nd$, and is zero otherwise. Since $17$ is relatively prime to $24$, the surviving shifts are
$d=(24/g)r$, $0\le r<g$.

The remaining quadratic factor is
$$
 \exp\left(-\pi i\,17(N/g)(24/g)r^2\right)=1,
$$
because $24/g$ is even. Thus the remaining finite character sum gives
$$
 |H(L)|^2=24g\,{\bf1}_{\{g\mid L\}},
$$
and hence
$$
 \boxed{
 |K_N(x,y)|^2=\frac gN\,{\bf1}_{\{g\mid x-17y\}}.
 }
 \tag{4.3}
$$
Each row contains exactly $N/g$ allowed entries. Its squared norm is one, and (3.7) implies $|\rho_N|=1$ and unitarity of $K_N$. In particular $S_N=H(0)$ has squared modulus $24g$.

For $3\mid N$, the support condition is equivalently $3\mid x+y$, since $17\equiv-1\pmod3$. The formula therefore retains the exact sparse support instead of making an incorrect nondegenerate Gaussian assertion at those dimensions.

## 5. Gauss norm, including the degenerate original coefficient

For any odd $L\ge1$ and integer $A$, set
$$
 G_L(A)=\sum_{z=0}^{L-1}e_L(Az^2),\qquad h=\gcd(A,L).
$$
For $L=1$, use $e_1=1$ and $G_1(A)=1$. For all odd $L$, expanding the squared modulus gives
$$
 |G_L(A)|^2
 =\sum_{d=0}^{L-1}e_L(Ad^2)
                         \sum_{z=0}^{L-1}e_L(2Adz).
 \tag{5.1}
$$
Since $2$ is invertible, the inner sum is $L$ exactly when $L\mid Ad$. The surviving $d=(L/h)r$ have
$Ad^2/L=(A/h)(L/h)r^2\in\mathbb Z$.
There are $h$ such shifts, so
$$
 \boxed{|G_L(A)|^2=L\,\gcd(A,L).}
 \tag{5.2}
$$
Applied to $A=-3\,4^{-1}\pmod N$, this gives $|G_N|^2=Ng$, with the same $g\in\{1,3\}$ as in Section 4. In particular $G_N\ne0$. Together with $|S_N|^2=24g$, (1.2) gives $|\rho_N|=1$ directly as well.

## 6. Independent direct completion of squares

Multiplying the original two kernels first, without the image construction, gives
$$
 \sqrt N(M_N^2)_{xy}
 =\frac{e_N(-3\,8^{-1}(x^2+y^2))}{\sqrt N}
   \sum_{z\bmod N}e_N(-3\,4^{-1}z^2+4^{-1}(x+y)z).
 \tag{6.1}
$$

If $3\nmid N$, completing the square inside the finite ring is valid and gives
$$
 \sqrt N(M_N^2)_{xy}
 =\gamma_N e_N(48^{-1}F(x,y)),\qquad
 \gamma_N=G_N/\sqrt N,\quad |\gamma_N|=1.
 \tag{6.2}
$$
To check the constant, the completion contributes
$48^{-1}(x+y)^2$, and $-3\,8^{-1}$ is $-18\,48^{-1}$, giving $F$.

Let $\nu_{48}N\equiv-1\pmod{48}$. Taking
$48^{-1}=(1+\nu_{48}N)/48$ as an integer representative rewrites (6.2) as
$$
 \sqrt N(M_N^2)_{xy}
 =\gamma_N e^{2\pi iF(x,y)/(48N)}
                      e^{2\pi i\nu_{48}F(x,y)/48}.
 \tag{6.3}
$$
The last factor is $24$-periodic in the integer $x$. Its cyclic autocorrelation at a shift $j$ is a constant phase times
$\sum_{x=0}^{23}e_{24}(-17\nu_{48}jx)$.
The coefficient $-17\nu_{48}$ is a unit modulo $24$, so this sum vanishes unless $j=0$. Every coefficient of its normalized $24$-point Fourier transform consequently has modulus $1/\sqrt{24}$. This is an independent derivation of the equal branch moduli.

If $N=3L$, translating $z$ by $L$ in (6.1) shows that its finite sum vanishes unless $3\mid x+y$: its multiplier is a nontrivial third root otherwise. If $x+y=3s$, the sum reduces exactly to three copies of an $L$-term Gauss sum. Completing its now nondegenerate square gives
$$
 \sqrt N(M_N^2)_{xy}
 =\sqrt3\,\gamma_L\,{\bf1}_{\{3\mid x+y\}}\,
 e^{2\pi iF(x,y)/(48N)}
 e^{2\pi i\{-3\nu_8(x^2+y^2)/8+\nu_{16}s^2/16\}},
 \tag{6.4}
$$
where
$$
 \nu_8N\equiv-1\pmod8,\qquad
 \nu_{16}L\equiv-1\pmod{16},\qquad
 \gamma_L=G_L(-4^{-1})/\sqrt L.
$$
Equation (5.2) gives $|\gamma_L|=1$. When $L=1$, the reduced Gauss sum is $1$, $4^{-1}$ is the unique residue modulo one, and one may choose $\nu_{16}=15$; formula (6.4) remains valid.

For an explicit Fourier modulus check in this second case, set $x=-y+3s$. The last exponent in (6.4) becomes
$$
 -\frac{3\nu_8y^2}{4}
 +\frac{(\nu_{16}-54\nu_8)s^2}{16}
 +\frac{9\nu_8ys}{4}.
 \tag{6.5}
$$
It is periodic under $s\mapsto s+8$. The quadratic coefficient
$\nu_{16}-54\nu_8$ is odd, hence a unit modulo eight; also
$\nu_{16}\equiv3\nu_8\pmod8$, consistently with both congruences above.

The $24$-point Fourier transform now has only eight nonzero input positions $x=-y+3s$. After extracting constants, each coefficient is $\sqrt3/24$ times an eight-term chirp
$$
 \sum_{s=0}^{7}
 e^{2\pi i\{As^2/16+Bs/16\}},
 \quad A\ \hbox{odd},\quad B\ \hbox{even}.
$$
Its squared modulus is eight: the shift autocorrelation contains
$\sum_{s=0}^{7}e_8(Ajs)$, which vanishes for all $j\ne0$. Its modulus is therefore $\sqrt8$, and the coefficient modulus is again
$\sqrt3\sqrt8/24=1/\sqrt{24}$.

At $N=3$, the original $M_3$ is the positive three-point Fourier transform, so $M_3^2$ is parity. The formulas above give $\rho_3=e^{-\pi i/4}$, and the image kernel is exactly that phase times parity. Thus the smallest degenerate case is included, not removed by an asymptotic qualifier.

## 7. What this does and does not provide

Equations (1.5) and (1.7) are exact initial kernels for all odd dimensions. They supply $24$ analytic Gaussian phases and branch coefficients of identical modulus, with the congruence degeneration at $3\mid N$ represented by interference among those branches. They can be used as initial data in a separately proved two-kick propagation construction.

No estimate on $P(U_N^2)-J_N$, on long quantum traces, or on $H_{\rm diag}$ is implied by the Gaussian initial identity alone. Those require their own phase analysis and error estimates.

The companion high-precision verification script checks full squares of the originally defined $M_N$, the image kernel, both direct formulas (6.3)--(6.4), the modular Weyl covariances, the support formula, and the $24$-point coefficient transform. These checks verify normalization and finite identities; they do not replace the proof or assert an asymptotic spectral result.
